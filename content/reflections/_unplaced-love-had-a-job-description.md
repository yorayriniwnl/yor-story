The same action. Two different names. One is love. One is damage. The line between them is not what the parent feels. The line is what the child eventually needs — and whether the parent was paying enough attention to know when those two things stopped being the same.

The parent who lets go for a better future is described as loving. The parent who holds on is described as attached. But in the moment of holding on, the parent is not thinking "I am being attached." They are thinking "I love this person and I cannot watch them go." The feeling is indistinguishable from love. The outcome is not.

> Love, in the parenting context, eventually has to become about the other person's life — not about your continued role in it. The moment it becomes about the role is the moment it stops being love and starts being management.

Nobody tells you this at the beginning. The beginning is about survival. Feed this person, protect this person, teach this person the words for things. The love and the management are the same act. Then the child grows. The management has to shrink. The love stays.

But some parents do not get the memo. Or they get it and cannot act on it. So the management continues past its useful life — the opinions about the career, the comments about the partner, the strategic deployment of sacrifice as an emotional instrument. "After everything I did for you." This sentence is not love. It is a demand for repayment on an investment the child never agreed to make.

---

The tragedy is that most of it is genuine. The holding on is real love with the wrong expiry date. The sacrifice was real. The care was real. The inability to let go is also real, and it is also, from a certain angle, the most honest thing about them — proof that they meant it. They just cannot tell the difference between meaning it and owning it.

Parents who let go for a better future. Parents who hold on because they cannot imagine a future that is not partly theirs. The same love. Different timing. That is not a small difference. The entire shape of a child's adult life can hang on which one they got.
